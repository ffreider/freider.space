# 01 · Sputnik 1 and its rocket, 4 October 1957

## 1. What happened
- **Launch:** 4 October 1957, 19:28:34 UTC (22:28:34 Moscow time), from the Tyuratam range in Soviet Kazakhstan, today's Baikonur Cosmodrome. The rocket was a modified R-7 ICBM (NSSDCA; Siddiqi, NASA History; McDowell GCAT).
- **The satellite (PS-1):** a polished aluminium sphere 58 cm across, mass 83.6 kg, with four whip antennas 2.4–2.9 m long. It carried two transmitters on 20.005 and 40.002 MHz, sending alternating pulses of 0.3 s. That was the "beep" (NSSDCA).
- **Orbit:** first apogee about 947 km, period about 96 minutes (NASA History Office, Launius). The engine cut off early, which left the apogee about 80 km lower than planned (Siddiqi).
- **Silence:** the batteries failed after about three weeks. NASA dates the end of transmissions to 26 October (Uri, NASA 2022).
- **The rocket:** the R-7's core stage reached orbit as well. At magnitude 1 it was far brighter than the sphere, which NSSDCA puts at about magnitude 6. NSSDCA says the core stage was "actually the 'Sputnik' people saw from the ground". It re-entered on 1 December 1957, about 08:46 UT, over Mongolia (NSSDCA).
- **Re-entry of the sphere:** after about 1,400 orbits. NSSDCA and NASA give 4 January 1958; the catalogue gives 3 January (see §5).

## 2. People
- **Sergei Korolev**, chief designer at OKB-1, whose bureau built the R-7 and PS-1. During the countdown he said "Nobody will hurry us" (Siddiqi's translation).
- **Mikhail Tikhonravov** led the design group that proposed a simple satellite. *Not verified this session; check Siddiqi before using.*
- **Dwight D. Eisenhower**, US President, who played down the satellite in public on 9 October.
- **Lloyd Berkner**, US delegate to the IGY committee, announced the launch at a reception at the Soviet embassy in Washington (Launius).
- **Walter Sullivan**, *New York Times* reporter, passed on the news that night (Launius).

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
Anyone with a shortwave receiver could hear the satellite, and anyone outside on a clear evening could see its rocket. Radio amateurs recorded the signal and used the changing pitch of the beeps to follow its path. Smithsonian's volunteer Moonwatch teams turned out to track it. In the US the response was close to panic: Launius calls it "hysteria" with a "Pearl Harbor" effect. That pressure led to the founding of NASA in 1958 and to the National Defense Education Act. NSSDCA notes the satellite also gave first data on upper-atmosphere density, from how fast its orbit decayed, and on radio propagation through the ionosphere.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "Now, so far as the satellite itself is concerned, that does not raise my apprehensions, not one iota." Eisenhower at his news conference of 9 October 1957 (American Presidency Project transcript, verified).
- "Now, somehow, in some new way, the sky seemed almost alien." Lyndon B. Johnson, later recalling that night, as quoted in Launius, *Sputnik and the Origins of the Space Age* (NASA History). This is a memoir quote, not a 1957 statement.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"People watched Sputnik cross the sky."** Most of them saw the rocket core stage (NSSDCA; James Oberg, NBC News 2007). The sphere sat at the edge of naked-eye visibility.
- **"It orbited every 90 minutes."** NASA's own 2022 article says this. The period was about 96 minutes (Launius; the catalogue lists 96.10).
- **"It beeped until it fell."** It stopped transmitting after about three weeks and stayed in orbit, silent, for about two more months.
- **Mass in pounds:** sources vary between 183 and 184 lb. Use 83.6 kg.
