# 07b — Ferdinand 1: the first rocket from Andøya (1962)

**Not an orbital launch.** Ferdinand 1 was a sounding rocket. It went up about 100 km and came straight back down, so it has no NORAD catalogue entry.

## 1. What happened
- **Date and time:** 18 August 1962 at 07:09 local time (Thrane 2018). The UTC time is unverified. It is 06:09 UTC on standard time (CET, UTC+1), or 05:09 UTC if Norway was on summer time in 1962. I have not checked whether it was.
- **Place:** Oksebåsen, about 6 km from Andenes on Andøya in Vesterålen, at 69°17′N, 16°01′E. Thrane says the site lies "in the centre of the night-time auroral zone".
- **Rocket:** **Ferdinand 1**, a two-stage US **Nike-Cajun**.
- **Altitude:** 101.5 km according to Thrane; Store norske leksikon (SNL) and Teknisk Ukeblad give about 102 km.
- **Payload:** a radio-wave experiment that measured electron density and how often electrons collided with neutral particles in the lower ionosphere. It also carried instruments to track the rocket's trajectory. Photometers and particle detectors were added on later flights.
- **The wider programme:** a three-way agreement between NTNF (Norway's research council for science and technology), the Danish Ionospheric Laboratory and NASA. It covered eight sounding rockets: six from Andøya and two from Wallops Island.

## 2. People
- **Finn Lied** (director of FFI, the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment) and Prof. **Svein Rosseland** (University of Oslo) started NTNF's space committee in January 1960.
- **Bjørn Landmark** (FFI) and **S. Fred Singer** (US) toured northern Norway in 1960 and recommended Andøya.
- **Egil Eriksen** (FFI) was at the launch panel for Ferdinand 1, as shown in Thrane's Fig. 1.
- **Odd Dahl** and **Asbjørn Søreide** of the Christian Michelsen Institute (CMI) in Bergen built the launcher.
- FFI ran the launch, built the payload structure and handled tracking. NASA supplied the telemetry and firing panel. Danish and Norwegian scientists built the experiments.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- Northern Norway's shortwave radio links failed when the aurora disturbed the ionosphere. Thrane notes the "great practical consequences for radio communication and navigation". SNL says the aim was to improve long-distance radio.
- SNL treats this launch as the start of Norway as a space nation. Andøya grew into a range used by many countries. It also led to ALOMAR, the SvalRak launch site on Svalbard, and today's Andøya Space.
- Thrane says the name Ferdinand, after the peaceful bull in the children's story, was chosen partly to signal peaceful research during the Cold War.

## 5. Objects to highlight
None in the catalogue, because sounding rockets never orbit. Optional link to the present day: ASBM-1 and ASBM-2 (NORAD 60422 and 60423), Space Norway's Arctic broadband satellites launched in 2024. They are in the catalogue but outside the 1962–1969 era.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Norway launched a satellite in 1962."** No, it was a suborbital sounding rocket.
- **"It photographed or measured the aurora directly."** The first payload measured radio propagation and electron density. Optical and particle instruments came on later Ferdinand flights. The launch was also at 07:09 in August daylight, with no visible aurora.
- **"A purely Norwegian rocket."** The rocket was American (Nike-Cajun), the payload was Danish–Norwegian, and the telemetry came from NASA.
- **Altitude:** sources give 101.5 or 102 km. Say "about 100 km".
