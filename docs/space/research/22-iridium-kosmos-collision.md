# 22 · Iridium 33 and Kosmos 2251 collide (10 February 2009)

## 1. What happened
- **When and where:** 10 February 2009 at **16:56 UTC**, over extreme northern Siberia at about **790 km** altitude (NASA ODQN 13-2). CelesTrak's SOCRATES had predicted the closest approach for between 16:55:59.670 and 16:55:59.990 UTC.
- **What:** **Iridium 33** was a working U.S. commercial communications satellite of about 560 kg, in an 86.4° orbit. **Kosmos 2251** was a decommissioned Russian military communications satellite (Strela-2M class) of about 900 kg, in a 74.0° orbit, launched from Plesetsk on 16 June 1993. Their orbital planes crossed at nearly a right angle, and they hit at a relative speed of **more than 11 km/s** (ODQN).
- **Warning that wasn't one:** SOCRATES's report of 10 Feb, 15:02 UTC, predicted a 584 m miss. That was not the closest approach predicted for any Iridium satellite that week (CelesTrak).
- **Debris at the time:** 823 larger fragments were catalogued by the end of March 2009 (ODQN). Radars at Haystack and Goldstone confirmed many more small pieces.
- **Debris now (local SATCAT, data to 16 Oct 2026):**
  - IRIDIUM 33 DEB (1997-051 prefix): **656 catalogued, 115 still in orbit.**
  - COSMOS 2251 DEB (1993-036 prefix): **1,714 catalogued, 608 still in orbit.** Two older "SL-8 DEB" pieces under the same prefix are excluded.
  - **Combined: 2,370 fragments catalogued, 723 still in orbit.** Both parent bodies are still catalogued with no decay date.

## 2. People
- **Iridium Satellite LLC** operated the constellation, which had 70 satellites in the operational altitude regime (ODQN). It is widely reported that an in-orbit spare took Iridium 33's place. I did not verify that from a primary source.
- **T.S. Kelso** (CelesTrak) ran SOCRATES, the public close-approach screening service that had flagged the pair, and published the first detailed reconstruction.
- **NASA ODPO**, then led by chief scientist Nicholas L. Johnson, characterised the debris clouds for the U.S. civil space programme.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- It showed that a collision between two satellites, long predicted by debris modellers, could actually happen. It happened in one of the busiest altitude bands: ODPO counted about 3,300 other catalogued objects passing through the Iridium altitude regime many times a day.
- Afterwards, U.S. Strategic Command's JSpOC expanded routine conjunction screening from about 120 manoeuvrable satellites to all active ones, according to U.S. Air Force news releases. This became the free collision-warning service operators worldwide now rely on. *(Search excerpts only; I could not open the primary Air Force pages.)*
- The event became the standard example of why operators share orbit data.

## 4. Primary-source quotes

- "This is the first time two satellites are known to have collided on orbit." — *T.S. Kelso, CelesTrak, "Iridium 33/Cosmos 2251 Collision"*.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"The first collision in space":** ODPO lists it as the *fourth* known accidental hypervelocity collision between catalogued objects. An earlier example is the French satellite Cerise, hit by a rocket fragment in 1996. It was the first between two *intact* satellites.
- **"Kosmos 2251 was active / a weapon":** It was a derelict communications satellite, thought to have stopped working about two years after launch.
- **Time and altitude:** Accounts vary between 776, 789 and 790 km, and between 16:55:59 and 16:56 UTC. Use "about 790 km, 16:56 UTC" (ODPO).
- **"Nobody saw it coming":** The pair appeared in public screening for days beforehand, with predicted misses of 117 m to 1.8 km. The data were too imprecise to act on.
