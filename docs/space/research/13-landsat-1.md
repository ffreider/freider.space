# 13 · Landsat 1 (ERTS-1), 23 July 1972

## 1. What happened
- **23 July 1972:** ERTS-A launched on a Delta (Delta 0900) from Vandenberg AFB, California. It was renamed ERTS-1 once it reached orbit. NASA announced the change of name to **Landsat 1 on 14 January 1975** (NASA caption, Commons file below).
- **Orbit:** sun-synchronous, near-polar, about 917 km, 103-minute period (14 orbits a day), 18-day repeat cycle (USGS).
- **Instruments:** a three-camera Return Beam Vidicon (RBV), a four-band Multispectral Scanner (MSS) and a data-collection system relaying readings from ground stations (NSSDCA). The RBV was meant to be the main instrument. It was shut down after it caused an electrical transient (NASA dates the power surge to 6 Aug 1972), and the MSS proved the better sensor.
- **25 July 1972:** first image, an MSS scene of Dallas–Fort Worth, Texas. USGS calls it "the very first image in the Landsat archive".
- **6 January 1978:** switched off. NSSDCA says drift in the orbital plane left the craft in almost constant sunlight, and it overheated. It was designed to last one year and ran for about six.
- **Continuity:** Landsats 2–9 followed. Landsat 6 (1993) did not reach orbit. Landsat 5 (launched 1 March 1984) returned data for 28 years 10 months, which Guinness recognised as a record in 2013. USGS made the archive free in 2008. Its figures: 25,000 scenes delivered in FY2001 (the best year before the policy) against more than 1 million downloaded in FY2009.

## 2. People
- **Virginia T. Norwood** (Hughes Aircraft) designed the MSS. NASA calls her the "Mother of Landsat". She died on 26 March 2023, aged 96.
- **Stewart Udall**, Secretary of the Interior, announced the Interior Department's satellite programme, Project EROS, on 21 September 1966. The announcement pushed NASA to start ERTS in 1967.
- **William T. Pecora**, Director of USGS, persuaded Udall to make that announcement.
- **Stanley C. Freden** (NASA Goddard) was the Landsat 1 project scientist.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
For the first time, an orbiting satellite was built specifically to map land surfaces again and again in calibrated spectral bands. NASA's Landsat 1 page lists early uses: a nautical chart edition built from Landsat data (1976), monitoring a frozen Chesapeake Bay (1977), and geologic mapping in support of plate tectonics. The unbroken record since 1972 lets scientists measure change over decades: deforestation, urban growth, glacier retreat and crop area. Landsat MSS datasets were added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2011 (USGS).

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Landsat 1 launched under that name."** It launched as ERTS-A, became ERTS-1 in orbit, and was named Landsat in 1975.
- **"The first Earth-imaging satellite."** Weather satellites (TIROS 1, 1960) and spy satellites came earlier. Landsat 1 was the first satellite designed for studying Earth's land resources.
- **Mass:** NSSDCA gives 1,800 kg and USGS about 953 kg. The figures disagree, so do not print either until one is confirmed.
- **"The RBV was the key camera."** The MSS was the instrument that succeeded.
