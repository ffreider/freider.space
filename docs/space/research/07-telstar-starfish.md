# 07 — Telstar, Ariel and Starfish Prime (1962)

## 1. What happened
- **Ariel 1** (UK's first satellite; also the first international satellite) launched 26 April 1962 on a Thor-Delta from Cape Canaveral. Built by NASA Goddard, carrying British experiments on the Sun's effect on the ionosphere.
- **Starfish Prime**, 9 July 1962, 09:00:09 UTC (still 8 July in Honolulu): a 1.4-megaton US warhead detonated at about 400 km altitude above Johnston Island in the Pacific. It was launched on a Thor missile. Stassinopoulos (NASA, 2015) says the blast put about 10^29 fission electrons into the magnetosphere. That made the inner Van Allen belt "several orders of magnitude" more intense.
- **Telstar 1** launched the next day, 10 July 1962, 08:35 UTC, on a Thor-Delta from Cape Canaveral. Bell Labs built it for AT&T, and AT&T paid NASA for the launch.
- **First TV:** on 11 July (US time; 12 July in France) Telstar carried test pictures from Andover, Maine, to Pleumeur-Bodou in France. The first public live transatlantic programme went out on 23 July 1962. The Smithsonian says "several hundred million" people in 16 nations watched.
- **Satellites lost:** Telstar's command channel failed in November 1962 from radiation damage to its transistors. It was revived in December, and its transmitter failed for good on 21 February 1963. Gombosi et al. (2017) table the damage from the belt:
  - Ariel 1: failed 5 August 1962 from solar-cell damage
  - Transit 4B: failed 2 August 1962
  - TRAAC: failed 12 August 1962
  - Also damaged: Kosmos 5, OSO-1, Explorer 14 and Explorer 15
- Telstar was only usable for about 20 minutes per 2½-hour orbit. It was in a low, elliptical orbit, not a geostationary one.

## 2. People
- **John R. Pierce** (Bell Labs) proposed orbital radio relays in 1955 and pushed Telstar forward.
- **Eugene F. O'Neill** (Bell Labs) was Telstar project director.
- **John F. Kennedy** opened his 23 July press conference by talking about Telstar. Europe's feed actually joined later, partway through an answer on Berlin.
- **E. G. Stassinopoulos** (NASA Goddard) later reconstructed the Starfish radiation dose on spacecraft.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- European viewers saw a live American press conference, and US viewers saw live Europe, for the first time without film being flown across the ocean.
- The EMP from Starfish reached Hawaii, about 1,450 km away. It knocked out roughly 300 streetlights and damaged a telephone microwave link. Source: Wikipedia, citing a Sandia report by Vittitoe (1989). The 300 figure has not been checked against that report.
- Engineers learned to radiation-harden satellites, and Telstar 2 (1963) was built to survive the belts. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty ended atmospheric and space nuclear tests.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "The achievement of the communications satellite while only a prelude already throws open to us the vision of an era of international communications." Kennedy, statement of 11 July 1962 (American Presidency Project). This is 23 words.
- "…resulting in the loss of seven satellites within months after the test, primarily from solar cell damage." Stassinopoulos, NASA/GSFC, 2015.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Telstar was the first communications satellite."** Wrong. SCORE (1958) and Echo 1 (1960) came first, and Courier 1B (1960) was an earlier active repeater. Telstar was the first to relay live transatlantic TV. It was also the first privately funded payload.
- **"Telstar was geostationary."** No, it was in low elliptical orbit with short passes.
- **"Telstar was destroyed by the blast."** No. It was launched after the blast and slowly degraded in the artificial belt.
- **"Kennedy's Telstar speech was seen in Europe."** The presidency transcript notes that the transmission to Europe began after his Telstar remarks.
- **Ariel 1's end date varies by source.** Wikipedia gives last contact as 9 July 1962. The peer-reviewed Gombosi table gives the failure as 5 August 1962, with intermittent operation later. Use the Gombosi date.
