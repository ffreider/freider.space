# 04 · Vanguard 1, 17 March 1958

## 1. What happened
- **Before it:** on 6 December 1957, Vanguard TV-3 rose about 4 feet off the pad, lost thrust and exploded on live television. The press called it "Flopnik" and "Kaputnik" (NASA, Uri 2017). On 31 January 1958 the Army's Explorer 1 reached orbit first.
- **Launch:** 17 March 1958, 12:15:41 UT, from Cape Canaveral (LC-18A), on Vanguard TV-4. The three-stage rocket was built for the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL). Third-stage injection came at 12:26:21 into a 654 × 3,969 km orbit with a 134.27-minute period at 34.25° (NSSDCA).
- **Satellite:** an aluminium sphere of **1.46 kg** and **16.5 cm** (NSSDCA), with six 30 cm antennas. It carried two transmitters. The 108 MHz telemetry transmitter ran on mercury batteries. The 108.03 MHz Minitrack beacon ran on **six silicon solar cells**, each about 5 cm square, giving about 1 W in total at 10% efficiency (NSSDCA).
- **First:** Vanguard 1 was the first satellite powered by solar cells (NSSDCA; NRL).
- **Going silent:** the battery transmitter stopped in **June 1958** (NSSDCA). The solar-powered beacon ran until **May 1964**, and the last signals were received at Quito, Ecuador (NSSDCA). Since then it has been tracked optically and by radar.
- **Lifetime:** it was first expected to stay up for about 2,000 years. Solar radiation pressure and drag at high solar activity cut the estimate to about **240 years** (NSSDCA).

## 2. People
- **John P. Hagen**, NRL astronomer and director of Project Vanguard. *His role is well known but was not re-checked against a primary source this session.*
- **John A. O'Keefe**, **Ann Eckels** (later Ann E. Bailie) and **R. K. Squires** analysed Vanguard 1's orbit and published the "pear-shaped" Earth result (*Science* 129, 565, 1959).
- **Hans Ziegler** of the Army Signal Corps is widely credited with pushing for solar cells on Vanguard. *Not verified; check before use.*

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- **Solar power:** six small cells kept a radio on for six years, compared with weeks on batteries. Nearly every satellite since has been solar-powered.
- **The shape of the Earth:** small, periodic changes in its orbit showed a third-degree term in Earth's gravity field. Earth is slightly "pear-shaped", with the Southern Hemisphere a little fuller than the Northern (O'Keefe et al. 1959). That improved geodesy, the science of measuring Earth's shape and gravity, which later navigation and mapping depend on.
- **Upper atmosphere:** the transmitters were also used to measure total electron content in the ionosphere (NSSDCA).

- Vanguard 1 is the **oldest human-made object still in orbit**. Sputnik 1, Sputnik 2, Explorer 1 and their rocket bodies (NORAD 1–4) have all decayed, so NORAD 5 is the lowest catalogue number still up. Its rocket stage, NORAD 16, is also still in orbit. It is the oldest rocket body in orbit, launched on the same day.
- The current apogee (3,819 km) is lower than the 1958 apogee (3,969 km), which shows the slow decay.
- NORAD 69999 is in the high "analyst/new" number range. Treat it as a recently catalogued fragment. *How and when it was catalogued is not verified.*

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"The second satellite ever."** It was the **fourth** satellite to reach orbit (after Sputniks 1 and 2 and Explorer 1) and the second American one.
- **"It still transmits"** is wrong. It has been silent since May 1964.
- **How long the batteries lasted:** NRL says about 20 days, and NSSDCA says the battery transmitter stopped in June 1958. NRL also says the solar cells ran "another seven years". The last signal in May 1964 is just over six years after launch.
- **Size:** NSSDCA gives 16.5 cm. Other sources give 15.2 cm (6 in). I could not resolve which figure is right.
