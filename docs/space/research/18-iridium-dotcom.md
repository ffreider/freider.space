# 18 · Iridium and the 1990s constellation bubble

## 1. What happened
- **5 May 1997:** the first five Iridium satellites launched on a Delta II from Vandenberg. Between 1997 and 2002, Iridium satellites went up on Delta II, Proton and Long March rockets. The catalogue holds 95 first-generation Iridium payloads launched in that period, which matches the "95 satellites launched between 1997 and 2002" figure SpaceNews attributes to Jonathan McDowell. The working constellation was 66 satellites in near-polar orbits about 780 km up (inclination 86.4° in the catalogue).
- **30 October 1998:** Vice President Al Gore made a ceremonial call from the White House Rose Garden. **1 November 1998:** commercial service began.
- **13 August 1999:** Iridium LLC filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, nine months after launch, after defaulting on about $1.55 billion in bank loans. Service was wound down in March 2000.
- **Late 2000:** Dan Colussy's new company, Iridium Satellite LLC, bought the assets for **$25 million**. The bankruptcy court approved the sale in November 2000, and some sources date the closing to December. A US Department of Defense contract helped the rescue.
- **Globalstar:** the first four satellites launched on 14 February 1998. Twelve were lost when a Zenit rocket failed in September 1998. Commercial service began between late 1999 and February 2000 (sources differ). The company filed for Chapter 11 on 15 February 2002. It used 48 satellites at about 1,400 km and 52° inclination.
- **Teledesic:** an "Internet-in-the-Sky" backed by Craig McCaw, Bill Gates, Boeing and Prince Alwaleed bin Talal. It was announced in 1994 with 840 satellites, cut to 288 and then to 30 (the 840/288 figures come from secondary sources). Its only satellite was the test craft T1/BATSAT, launched 26 February 1998 on a Pegasus XL, which re-entered in 2000. Work stopped on **1 October 2002**, and the licence went back to the FCC in July 2003.
- **Iridium flares:** each first-generation satellite had three door-sized polished antennas. Sunlight off them gave predictable flashes up to about magnitude −8, much brighter than Venus. The last working first-generation satellite (SV097) was deorbited in December 2019, ending predictable flares. About 30 dead units remain in orbit.

## 2. People
- **Dan Colussy:** former Pan Am president who bought Iridium out of bankruptcy.
- **Al Gore:** made the ceremonial first call.
- **Gilbert M. Grosvenor:** received that call; a great-grandson of Alexander Graham Bell.
- **Craig McCaw:** mobile-phone pioneer who founded Teledesic.
- **Todd Wolfenbarger:** Teledesic spokesman in 2002.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- For the first time a hand-held phone could reach anyone from anywhere, including the poles and the open sea. The handsets were expensive and the market was tiny: one case study counts about 10,000 subscribers in March 1999.
- After the rescue, Iridium became essential for militaries, ships, expeditions and Arctic users.
- The failures of Iridium, Globalstar and Teledesic made investors wary of large low-orbit constellations for over a decade, until Starlink.
- Flare-spotting became a popular amateur pastime; Heavens-Above published predictions.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- "Iridium has 77 satellites." The name comes from element 77, after an early 77-satellite design. The working constellation was 66 plus spares.
- "Iridium's satellites were burned up in 2000." The plan to deorbit them was cancelled by the rescue. They flew until the 2017–2019 replacement.
- "Gore's call was on 1 November." The ceremonial call was 30 October; commercial service started 1 November.
- One NYU case study says Gore's call went to Iridium CEO Edward Staiano. The White House release names Grosvenor.
