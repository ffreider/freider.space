# 21 · Fengyun-1C anti-satellite test (11 January 2007)

## 1. What happened
- **When:** 11 January 2007. CelesTrak (T.S. Kelso) puts the most likely intercept time at **22:26:10 UTC**. *Aviation Week* reported 22:28 UTC. Local time in China was already 12 January.
- **What:** A ballistic interceptor launched near the Xichang launch centre in Sichuan struck **Fengyun-1C**, a retired Chinese polar-orbiting weather satellite launched in 1999. NASA's Orbital Debris Program Office (ODPO) gave its orbit as **845 × 865 km** at 98.6° inclination and its mass as **960 kg**. Other sources give 863 km and 880 kg. The interceptor is often named as the SC-19, but China has not published technical details.
- **Debris, as reported at the time:** Two months later, more than 1,200 fragments had been officially catalogued and nearly 400 more were being tracked (ODQN 11-2, April 2007). By 22 June 2012 the count was 3,312 catalogued, of which 256 had decayed (CelesTrak). ODPO first estimated up to 35,000 fragments of 1 cm or larger. By January 2008 it had raised that to more than 150,000 (ODQN 12-1, as cited by CelesTrak).
- **Debris now (local SATCAT, data to 16 Oct 2026):** There are **3,534 DEB objects** under 1999-025: 3,531 named "FENGYUN 1C DEB" and 3 named "CZ-4B DEB". **2,314 of them have no decay date** and are still in orbit (2,311 of the Fengyun pieces). Decay was slow at first: 22 pieces in 2007 and 22 in 2008. It sped up in 2023–2025 (142, 172 and 100 pieces) as solar maximum heated the upper atmosphere.
- Fengyun-1C remains the largest single debris-creating event on record by catalogued fragment count.

## 2. People
- **China's government and the PLA** carried out the test and stayed silent for 12 days. On 23 January 2007, Foreign Ministry spokesman **Liu Jianchao** confirmed it and said it was not aimed at any country. English versions of his words differ between outlets (see §4).
- **T.S. Kelso** (CelesTrak/CSSI) published the first public orbital reconstruction and has kept up the long-running debris count.
- **Nicholas L. Johnson** was NASA's chief scientist for orbital debris at the time. ODPO's newsletter published the official U.S. civil assessment.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- The cloud spans roughly 200 km to almost 4,000 km in altitude and crosses the orbits of hundreds of working satellites, including the human-spaceflight band (ODQN). Within a year, Fengyun pieces accounted for over 28% of all predicted close approaches within 5 km in one CelesTrak SOCRATES weekly run (22 January 2008).
- Satellite operators, including those running weather and Earth-observation satellites people rely on daily, now have to screen against and sometimes dodge these fragments.
- The test came one month before the UN COPUOS subcommittee adopted debris-mitigation guidelines on 21 February 2007. Guideline 4 asks states to avoid intentional destruction that creates long-lived debris (ODQN 11-2).

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Altitude 865 km":** 865 km was the apogee. ODPO gives the orbit as 845 × 865 km. "About 860 km" is safer.
- **"3,000+ fragments created":** That figure is the catalogued count of objects larger than about 5–10 cm. The number of pieces 1 cm or larger is estimated in the tens to hundreds of thousands.
- **"The debris is gone":** About two-thirds of the catalogued pieces were still in orbit nearly 20 years later.
- **"First ASAT test":** The U.S. and USSR tested ASAT systems in the Cold War, including the U.S. destruction of Solwind P78-1 in 1985. 2007 was the first such test in about two decades and the dirtiest.
