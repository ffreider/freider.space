# 14 · Navstar 1, the first GPS satellite (22 February 1978)

## 1. What happened
- **22 February 1978:** Navstar 1 (US military designation OPS 5111) launched on an Atlas F from Vandenberg AFB, California. Wikipedia gives liftoff as 23:44 UTC from SLC-3E; I have not confirmed the time against a NASA or USAF primary source. It was the first of eleven Block I development satellites built by Rockwell. Ten reached orbit. The satcat has no "Navstar 7", which fits the 1981 launch failure, but I have not sourced that failure independently.
- **Orbit (current catalogue elements):** 20,625 × 20,184 km, 64.4°, period 727 minutes, about half a sidereal day. Navstar 1 is still up there, dead.
- **14 February 1989:** the first Block II operational satellite was launched: NAVSTAR 13 (USA 35), 1989-013A, NORAD 19802, from Cape Canaveral.
- **8 December 1993:** Initial Operational Capability was declared, with 24 Block I/II/IIA satellites in their assigned orbits (USNO).
- **1995:** Full Operational Capability. Air Force Space Command declared it as of **27 April 1995** and announced it publicly on **17 July 1995**. Sources use either date, so the story should say which one it means.
- **1 September 1983:** a Soviet fighter shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 after it strayed into Soviet airspace. On **16 September 1983** the White House put out a "Statement by the Principal Deputy Press Secretary – Civilian Airlines to be Equipped with GPS" (Reagan Library, Press Releases, Box 65, #5012). The principal deputy press secretary then was Larry Speakes.
- **2 May 2000, about 04:05 UTC** (just after midnight US Eastern time): all GPS satellites stopped adding the deliberate Selective Availability (SA) error. A US government data page measured one station's 95% error radius before and after: **45.0 m** on 1 May and **6.3 m** on 3 May. In 2007 the government decided that GPS III would be built without SA.

## 2. People
- **Col. Bradford Parkinson** (USAF) led the Navstar GPS Joint Program Office. Over Labor Day weekend 1973 at the Pentagon, he and his team worked out the system concept that was approved.
- **Roger Easton** (Naval Research Laboratory) led Timation, which flew atomic clocks in space ahead of GPS.
- **Ivan Getting** (Aerospace Corporation) is widely credited as an early advocate of satellite navigation. I have not confirmed his role from a primary source.
- **Bill Clinton** ordered SA switched off. **Ronald Reagan's** White House made the 1983 civil-aviation commitment.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
GPS began as military navigation and became free infrastructure used worldwide. Once SA was switched off, consumer receivers improved overnight from roughly the size of a football field to a few metres (see the figures above). That made car navigation, geocaching and eventually smartphone mapping practical. The atomic-clock timing signal now synchronises telecom networks, power grids and financial timestamps. That last point is widely reported; for a primary source, try GPS.gov's "timing" page.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Reagan made GPS free for everyone in 1983."** The 1983 statement came from the press office, was about civilian *aircraft*, and promised access once the system was operational. It did not end SA. The "free of direct user fees" guarantee came later, with Clinton's March 1996 Presidential Decision Directive, which his 2000 statement cites.
- **"GPS was switched on for civilians in 2000."** Civilians had the degraded signal throughout the 1980s and 1990s. What changed in 2000 was accuracy.
- **"GPS was finished in 1993."** That was IOC. FOC came in 1995.
- **"Navstar 1 was the first navigation satellite."** Transit (US Navy) has been navigating since the 1960s.
