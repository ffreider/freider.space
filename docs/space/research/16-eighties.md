# 16 · The 1980s: Shuttle, Mir and a crowded geostationary ring

## 1. What happened
- **Space Shuttle, STS-1:** Columbia launched from Pad 39A, Kennedy Space Center, on **12 April 1981 at 7:00:03 a.m. EST (12:00:03 UTC)**. Crew: John W. Young (commander) and Robert L. Crippen (pilot). The flight lasted 37 orbits, 2 d 6 h 20 min 53 s. Columbia landed on 14 April 1981 at Edwards AFB, Runway 23 (NASA).
- **The shuttle as a launcher for geostationary satellites:** On STS-5 (Columbia, launched 11 Nov 1982) the shuttle deployed two commercial communications satellites, SBS 3 and Anik C3. The catalogue lists them as 1982-110B and 1982-110C, sharing a designator with the orbiter (1982-110A).
- **Mir:** the core module (base block, DOS-7) launched on a Proton from Baikonur on **19 February 1986 at 21:28:23 UTC**, which was already **20 February in Moscow**. Both dates appear in sources. NASA's *Mir Hardware Heritage* (RP-1357) uses each in different places. The first crew, Leonid Kizim and Vladimir Solovyov, arrived on **15 March 1986**. Mir re-entered on 23 March 2001 (catalogue decay date).
- **Geostationary belt:** I counted satcat objects with PERIOD between 1430 and 1450 min that had been launched and had not decayed by each date:
  - **31 Dec 1980: 121** (86 payloads, 18 rocket bodies, 17 debris)
  - **31 Dec 1989: 259** (192 payloads, 48 rocket bodies, 19 debris)
  
  **Caveats:** (a) PERIOD is today's value, not the value in 1980 or 1989. Satellites since boosted into graveyard orbits fall outside the window (Astra 1A is now 1464 min, Ekran 10 is 1515 min), so these counts are *lower bounds*. A looser filter (perigee ≥ 30,000 km, period 1300–1600 min) gives 273 and 543. (b) Debris inherits its parent's launch date, so a few "debris" pieces may come from later breakups. Counting only payloads and rocket bodies gives **104 → 240**. Either way, the population roughly doubled during the decade.
- **TV from space:** Astra 1A (SES, Luxembourg) launched on an Ariane on 11 Dec 1988 (00:33 UTC, Kourou). It began broadcasting on 5 Feb 1989, carrying Sky Television's four channels to dishes across Europe.
- **ITU slots:** The ITU's 1977 World Administrative Radio Conference adopted a plan for direct broadcasting satellites. A two-session conference followed: **WARC ORB-85** (Geneva, 8 Aug–15 Sep 1985) and **ORB-88** (29 Aug–6 Oct 1988). It produced an allotment plan for the fixed-satellite service and completed the broadcasting-satellite plan (ITU History Portal). The principle behind it is that the geostationary orbit is a limited natural resource, to be used rationally and efficiently with equitable access for developing countries. That principle is now in ITU Constitution Art. 44, wording as amended in 1998.
- **Graveyard orbits:** dead geostationary satellites can be boosted a few hundred km above GEO. ITU-R Recommendation S.1003 (1993) implied about 300 km. The IADC formula agreed in 1997 is 235 km + 1000·C_R·A/m. Both formal guidelines come *after* this era. I have not verified when reorbiting became routine during the 1980s.

## 2. People
- **John Young** commanded STS-1. He had walked on the Moon during Apollo 16.
- **Robert Crippen** piloted STS-1 on his first spaceflight.
- **Leonid Kizim and Vladimir Solovyov** were Mir's first crew. They arrived on Soyuz T-15.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
The shuttle never reached the launch rate or the costs that were promised. I have no primary figures to hand, so take care with numbers here. It did carry commercial geostationary satellites to orbit until the 1986 Challenger accident; the date is not checked here. Mir made continuous long-duration living in orbit routine. The filling of the geostationary ring is what put satellite TV dishes on European balconies, and it explains why orbital slots became diplomatic property.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **Mir's launch date.** 19 Feb is right in UTC and 20 Feb is right in Moscow time. Give the time zone.
- **"STS-1 was the shuttle's first flight."** It was the first *orbital* flight. Enterprise's approach and landing tests took place in 1977.
- **"Graveyard orbits were required in the 1980s."** There was no international guideline before ITU-R S.1003 in 1993.
- **"The ITU sells or assigns GEO slots."** The ITU coordinates frequency assignments and keeps the plans. States file for slots and sovereign operators use them.
