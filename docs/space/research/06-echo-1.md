# 06 · Echo 1, the balloon satellite, 12 August 1960

## 1. What happened
- **Launch:** 12 August 1960, 09:39:43 UTC (5:39 a.m. local), from LC-17A at Cape Canaveral, on Thor-Delta No. 2. It was the first successful Delta launch, after Delta 1 failed with Echo A-10 in May 1960 (GCAT; Hansen, NASA SP-4308).
- **Spacecraft:** a balloon **30.48 m (100 ft)** across, made of Mylar polyester 0.0127 mm (0.5 mil) thick and coated with vapour-deposited aluminium. It carried two 107.9 MHz tracking beacons powered by 70 solar cells and five nickel-cadmium batteries (NSSDCA). It was launched folded and inflated in orbit using a sublimating solid pressurant (Hansen).
- **First relay:** at **7:41 a.m.** local time, still on its first orbit, Echo reflected a recorded message from President Eisenhower. The signal went from JPL's Goldstone station in California to Bell Labs at Holmdel, New Jersey (Hansen; American Presidency Project note). Two-way coast-to-coast telephone calls followed, then signals between the US and France (Hansen).
- **Orbit:** roughly 1,000 miles up, by Eisenhower's figure ("approximately 1000 miles") and Hansen's.
- **Seen by millions (*the number is unmeasured*):** NASA released daily schedules of when and where to see it (Hansen).
- **Re-entry:** 24 May 1968 (NSSDCA). Echo kept its shape far longer than experts had predicted, and its large area made it a probe of air density and solar radiation pressure (NSSDCA; Hansen).

## 2. People
- **William J. O'Sullivan Jr.**, NACA/NASA Langley engineer, came up with the inflatable "satelloon". Hansen notes that colleagues dispute O'Sullivan's own heroic account of his role.
- **Walter Bressette**, Langley, "played a major role in the Echo program from start to finish" (Hansen, photo caption).
- **John R. Pierce**, director of electronics research at Bell Labs. He had argued in print for passive relay satellites, and Bell Labs provided the receiving station at Holmdel (Hansen).
- **Robert J. Mackey Jr.**, NASA Goddard, project manager (NSSDCA).

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
Echo was the first satellite many people knowingly saw: a bright, steady point crossing the sky, easier to pick out than any earlier satellite. It showed that a voice could be bounced across a continent and an ocean by way of orbit. Hansen calls this the demonstration that the rapid growth of worldwide satellite communications in the 1960s "depended upon". It was also a deliberate peacetime contrast with Sputnik, offered "freely" to any nation. Later in its life it was used for geodetic triangulation (NSSDCA).

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "The satellite balloon which has reflected these words may be used freely by any nation for similar experiments in its own interests." From Eisenhower's message recorded for transmission via Echo I, 12 August 1960 (American Presidency Project, verified).
- "Thanks for making me look up." From a 1968 poem, "To a Falling Star", sent to NASA by a young Canadian girl as Echo was about to re-enter. Quoted in Hansen, *Spaceflight Revolution* (NASA SP-4308). The author is not named.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **The Eisenhower quote.** Hansen (SP-4308) prints a condensed version ("…being carried forward for peaceful purposes…") that differs from the White House text. Quote the American Presidency Project version.
- **Mass:** NSSDCA gives 180 kg. Hansen gives 132 lb (about 60 kg) for the inflated sphere, including 30 lb of inflation chemicals. The two may be measuring different things; check which before printing a number.
- **"First communications satellite."** Echo was the first *passive* one, a reflector. SCORE (December 1958) had already broadcast a recorded Eisenhower message from orbit using an onboard recorder. *SCORE's details were not re-verified this session.*
- **"Echo is still up there."** The balloon re-entered in 1968. Only its rocket stage and debris remain.
