# 03 · Explorer 1 and the radiation belts, 31 January 1958

## 1. What happened
- **Launch:** 31 January 1958 at 10:48 p.m. EST, which was **1 February, 03:48 UTC**. It flew from Launch Complex 26A, Cape Canaveral, on a Jupiter-C (NSSDCA; NASA; GCAT gives 03:47:56 UTC).
- **Rocket:** a Redstone missile modified with three solid-fuel upper stages, built by the Army Ballistic Missile Agency (ABMA) in Huntsville. The final stage stayed attached to the satellite. NSSDCA lists the vehicle as "Jupiter C (Juno I)".
- **Satellite:** built by JPL. NSSDCA gives 13.97 kg; NASA's John Uri says 30 lb, with more than 18 lb of instruments. It carried a Geiger-counter cosmic-ray experiment designed by James Van Allen's group at the University of Iowa, a micrometeorite microphone and erosion gauges, temperature sensors and two transmitters (NASA).
- **The puzzle:** at times the Geiger counter read zero. Van Allen's team proposed that the tube was being *saturated* by radiation far stronger than expected. **Explorer 3** (launched 26 March 1958) carried a tape recorder, which allowed whole orbits to be recorded, and it confirmed the idea (Gurnett 2007).
- **Announcement:** the discovery was made public at the National Academy of Sciences in Washington on **1 May 1958** (Gurnett 2007).
- **Silence and re-entry:** the batteries died on 23 May 1958. Explorer 1 burned up over the Pacific on 31 March 1970 (NASA, Uri 2018).

## 2. People
- **James A. Van Allen** (1914–2006), University of Iowa physicist and principal investigator of the cosmic-ray instrument.
- **William H. Pickering**, JPL director, whose lab built the satellite and tracked it.
- **Wernher von Braun**, ABMA technical director, whose team adapted the rocket.
- **George Ludwig**, the Iowa graduate student who built much of the instrument. *Named in many accounts but not checked against a primary source this session.*
- **JPL's women "computers"** confirmed the orbit, which was higher than planned (NASA, Uri 2018). Susan Finley, who joined JPL in January 1958 in that role, appears in NASA's 60th-anniversary photos.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
This was the first American satellite, launched four months after Sputnik and two months after the televised Vanguard explosion of 6 December 1957. It restored some national confidence. Uri describes the radiation-belt finding as "considered one of the outstanding scientific discoveries of the IGY". It started the field now called magnetospheric physics. It was also a practical warning: crews and electronics passing through the belts need shielding, which shaped orbits for everything from Apollo to GPS. In 1958 Congress created NASA, and JPL moved to it.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Explorer 1 had a tape recorder."** NASA's own "Short History of Earth's Radiation Belts" says so. The Iowa account (Gurnett 2007) and the Smithsonian say the first satellite tape recorder flew on **Explorer 3**.
- **"Explorer 1 discovered both belts."** Explorer 1 and 3 found the inner region. NASA credits Explorer 4 and Pioneer 3 (1958) with finding the outer belt.
- **Rocket name:** "Jupiter-C" and "Juno I" are both used for the same rocket. It was a Redstone derivative, not a Jupiter missile.
- **Date:** 31 January (Florida time) and 1 February (UTC) are both correct.
