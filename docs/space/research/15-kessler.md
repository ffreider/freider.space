# 15 · Kessler & Cour-Palais, "the creation of a debris belt" (1978)

## 1. What happened
- **Paper:** Donald J. Kessler and Burton G. Cour-Palais, "Collision frequency of artificial satellites: The creation of a debris belt", *Journal of Geophysical Research* 83(A6): 2637–2646, June 1978 (Crossref gives the issue date as 1 June 1978). doi:10.1029/JA083iA06p02637. Both authors were at NASA Johnson Space Center, Houston (NASA NTRS record).
- **Argument (from the abstract):** as satellites accumulate, so do the chances that they collide. Each collision produces fragments, and those fragments raise the odds of further collisions. The result could be a self-sustaining "belt of debris around the earth", similar to theories of how the asteroid belt grew. The debris flux in such a belt "could exceed the natural meteoroid flux". Their model found the belt "could begin to form within this century" and become "a significant problem during the next century". Unseen fragments from explosions would shorten that timeline. Early launch constraints and operational procedures could delay it.
- **Specifics (Kessler's own 2009 summary):** the paper predicted that catalogued objects would start breaking up through random collisions around 2000, and that the catalogue would grow by about 510 objects a year. Actual growth was slower: fewer explosions after mitigation agreements, strong solar activity that dragged objects down, and fewer Soviet launches as the USSR declined.
- **The name:** Kessler writes that John Gabbard of NORAD coined "Kessler Syndrome" while talking to a reporter shortly after the paper came out, and that the reporter printed it. Kessler suggests the phrase may have spread through Jim Schefter's 1982 *Popular Science* article. Kessler says only "as far as I am aware", and I have found no account from Gabbard himself.
- **How it has fared:** On 10 February 2009, Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251 collided at 790 km altitude and 11.6 km/s. Kessler called this "the first clear example of what was predicted in 1978". He counts three earlier random collisions between catalogued objects since 1991, none of them catastrophic. NASA's later modelling, which Kessler cites, concludes that the low-orbit environment is already above a critical threshold: collision fragments will be produced faster than drag removes them, so cutting off the past sources of debris alone would probably not stop the population growing.

## 2. People
- **Donald J. Kessler** was a NASA JSC astrophysicist. NASA funded him from October 1979 to measure and model debris, work that led to what is now the NASA Orbital Debris Program Office.
- **Burton G. Cour-Palais** was a JSC co-author whose field was meteoroid and hypervelocity impact. I have not found a primary-source biography for him; check before saying more.
- **John Gabbard** worked at NORAD. He kept an unofficial record of breakups in orbit, and the "Gabbard plot" is named after him.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
The paper turned "space junk" from a curiosity into an engineering limit. Its findings fed into NASA's debris programme, the international Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC), the practice of venting leftover fuel from rocket stages, and the guideline that dead objects should leave orbit within 25 years. Today it shapes satellite shielding, collision-avoidance manoeuvres (including on the ISS), insurance, and the debate over megaconstellations.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "Satellite collisions would produce orbiting fragments, each of which would increase the probability of further collisions, leading to the growth of a belt of debris around the earth." (Kessler & Cour-Palais 1978, abstract, as given in NASA NTRS)
- "It was never intended to mean that the cascading would occur over a period of time as short as days or months." (Donald J. Kessler, "The Kessler Syndrome", 8 March 2009, on his own pages at aquarid.physics.uwo.ca)

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Kessler predicted a sudden chain reaction that would make orbit unusable within days."** He explicitly denies this (quote above). The process plays out over decades.
- **"Kessler coined the term."** By his own account, Gabbard did.
- **"The paper says we are already past the tipping point."** The 1978 paper did not address a critical threshold. That idea was studied more than ten years later (Kessler 2009).
- **"The 2009 collision was the first collision."** It was the first *catastrophic* accidental collision. Smaller ones came earlier.
