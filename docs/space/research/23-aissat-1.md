# 23 · AISSat-1, Norway's own ship-tracking satellite (12 July 2010)

## 1. What happened
- **Launch:** 12 July 2010, about **03:52 UTC**, on India's **PSLV-C15** from Sriharikota (Satish Dhawan Space Centre). It was a secondary payload. The main payload was Cartosat-2B, and AISSat-1 rode in the Toronto-run Nanosatellite Launch Service 6 (NLS-6) cluster.
- **The satellite:** A 20 × 20 × 20 cm cube of about 6 kg (SFL says 7 kg). Its job was to listen from orbit for **AIS** (Automatic Identification System) radio messages that ships broadcast with their identity, position, speed and heading. AIS is required for vessels over 300 gross tonnes. Land-based receivers cannot hear ships far out at sea. I have not verified a typical range figure.
- **First data:** FFI's own account says that on **13 July 2010 at 10:56**, red arrows marking ships began appearing on a monitor at the Norwegian Coastal Administration, with data relayed from AISSat-1.
- **Cost and lifespan:** Budget NOK 28 million, including launch and three years of operations (FFI). Design life was about three years. It operated for around ten years (SNL). FFI wrote in March 2022 that it "remains in operation". The local SATCAT now marks it non-operational, and I found no exact end date.
- **What came next:**
  - **AISSat-2:** 8 July 2014, Soyuz from Baikonur. Stopped operating October 2023 (SFL).
  - **AISSat-3:** Lost on 28 November 2017 when the Soyuz-2.1b/Fregat launching Meteor-M No. 2-1 from Vostochny failed.
  - **NORSAT-1 and NORSAT-2:** 14 July 2017, Soyuz-2.1a from Baikonur, both built by SFL. NorSat-1 also carried the CLARA solar radiometer and Langmuir probes. NorSat-2 carried an experimental VHF Data Exchange (VDE) payload.
  - **Later:** NORSAT-3 (2021) and NORSAT-4 (2025), with AISSat-4 launched on 30 March 2026.

## 2. People
- **FFI (Norwegian Defence Research Establishment)** proposed the AIS satellite to the Norwegian Space Centre board on 5 April 2005, managed the programme and operated the satellite. FFI's **Bjørn Narheim** made that 2005 proposal.
- **Norwegian Space Centre**, now the Norwegian Space Agency, funded it. **Terje Wahl** and **Frank Udnæs** were among its department directors.
- **UTIAS Space Flight Laboratory** (University of Toronto) built the bus and antenna and handled integration, launch and commissioning. **Kongsberg Seatex** built the AIS receiver, and **KSAT** ran the Svalbard ground station.
- **Trond Giske**, then Minister of Trade and Industry, marked the launch with a riff on Armstrong (see §4).

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- The Coastal Administration could suddenly see ships in the Barents and Norwegian Seas beyond the reach of its 44 land AIS stations. That helps search and rescue, fisheries control, oil-spill response and smuggling cases. An officer at the Norwegian Joint Headquarters said the first two years were like turning on the light in those seas (FFI).
- By 2020, KSAT said it was collecting over 500,000 AIS messages a day from more than 30,000 ships, and its data had helped expose "dark" fishing vessels.
- It showed that a small country could build useful national satellites cheaply. That approach led directly to the NorSat line.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "It's one small satellite but one giant leap for Norwegian space travel." *Trond Giske, quoted by FFI, "It was like turning on the light" (28 March 2022).*
- "The project showed us that it is possible to develop our own customised satellites at a manageable price." *Terje Wahl, Norwegian Space Agency, same FFI article.*

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Norway's first satellite":** Not quite. The SATCAT lists Norwegian-owned **THOR I** (1990-074A, a satellite bought in orbit), **THOR II** (1997), THOR III, THOR 5 and THOR 6, all commercial telecom satellites. AISSat-1 was Norway's first *national* satellite: state-funded, non-commercial and built to Norwegian requirements. The SNL encyclopedia and FFI word it this way.
- **Launch date:** FFI's 2022 article says "8 July 2010" in one paragraph and "launched two days earlier" (before 13 July) in another. 12 July 2010 UTC is correct.
- **"Built in Norway":** The payload was Norwegian. The satellite itself was built in Toronto.
