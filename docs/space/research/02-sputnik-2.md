# 02 · Sputnik 2 and Laika, 3 November 1957

## 1. What happened
- **Launch:** 3 November 1957, 02:30:42 UTC, from Tyuratam (Baikonur), on a modified R-7 (McDowell GCAT; NSSDCA). It flew less than a month after Sputnik 1.
- **Spacecraft:** a cone about 4 m high with a 2 m base and a mass of 508.3 kg, about six times Sputnik 1. Inside were radio transmitters, telemetry, two spectrophotometers for solar UV, X-ray and cosmic-ray measurements, and a separate sealed cabin for the dog **Laika**. A TV camera sent 100-line pictures of her at 10 frames per second (NSSDCA).
- **No way home:** the craft had no recovery system. Laika was always going to die in orbit (NASA, Uri 2022).
- **Overheating:** the cabin overheated. NASA's history essay (Harford) says it "overheated after failing to separate from its booster", which disabled the thermal control system. Royal Museums Greenwich says that from 1999 Russian sources placed her death on the **fourth orbit**. Smithsonian *Air & Space* cites a 2002 report from Moscow's Institute of Biomedical Problems (D. Malashenkov, presented at the 2002 World Space Congress in Houston): Laika died of overheating **a few hours** into the flight. I could not access the original paper; it is catalogued as an IAF abstract, 2002iaf..confE.288M.
- **Afterwards:** the batteries ran out around 10 November, and the craft re-entered on 14 April 1958 after about 2,570 orbits (NASA; RMG). That is about 162 days in orbit.

## 2. People
- **Sergei Korolev**, chief designer of OKB-1, ordered the second satellite built at short notice after Sputnik 1 succeeded (Harford, NASA History).
- **Vladimir Yazdovsky**, a Soviet space medicine lead, chose and trained the dogs. RMG quotes him calling Laika "quiet and charming".
- **Oleg Gazenko**, dog trainer and later director of the Institute of Biomedical Problems. Later accounts say that in 1993 he publicly regretted the flight. *I did not verify his exact words.*
- **Laika**, a mixed-breed stray from Moscow, was the first animal to orbit the Earth.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
Sputnik 2 showed that the R-7 could lift half a tonne. That reinforced American fears about Soviet missiles after Sputnik 1. It also showed that a mammal could survive launch and enter weightlessness alive, which was a step toward Gagarin's flight in 1961. The Soviet press stayed quiet about her fate, which drew protests from animal-welfare groups in the West. *The specifics of those protests are not verified here.* Laika became a lasting public symbol of what early spaceflight cost.

A US Air Force photo taken from Patrick AFB in March 1958 shows "Sputnik 2 and its rocket".

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **How long Laika lived.** Soviet statements at the time implied she lived for days, and some said she was put down painlessly. Later Russian disclosures from 1993, 1999 and 2002 say she died within hours, from overheating.
- **"Laika was the first animal in space."** She was the first to **orbit**. Animals had flown on suborbital rockets before. *I did not re-verify the specific earlier flights this session.*
- **Whether separation was planned.** Harford (NASA) says the capsule *failed* to separate. Other accounts, including Wikipedia, say Sputnik 2 was *not designed* to separate. The sources conflict, so do not assert either version without Siddiqi's *Challenge to Apollo* or a Russian primary source.
- **"Almost 200 days in orbit."** Launius's NASA essay says this. The catalogue dates give about 162 days.
- **Mass:** NASA gives 1,120 lb in one essay and 1,121 lb in another. Use 508.3 kg (NSSDCA).
