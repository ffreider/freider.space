# 11 — The first debris clouds (1961–1969)

## 1. What happened
- **Launch, 29 June 1961:** a Thor-Ablestar launched from Cape Canaveral (Air Force Eastern Test Range) carrying three satellites: Transit 4A and the joined pair Injun 1 / Solrad 3.
- **Breakup, about 77 minutes after orbital insertion:** NASA's history of on-orbit breakups (16th ed.) records the Ablestar upper stage exploding at 06:08 GMT at about 990 km altitude.
  - The stage's 378 MHz beacon stopped at 06:08:10.
  - About 100 kg of self-igniting propellant was still on board. This was the first Ablestar flight that did not vent its fuel tank.
  - A Baker-Nunn tracking camera at Organ Pass, New Mexico, photographed the breakup.
  - The assessed cause is "propulsion", and investigators recommended venting fuel on future flights.
- NASA counts 317 catalogued fragments from this event, 193 still in orbit as of May 2022. A 1964 reconstruction shows 201 fragments.
- **Other 1960s breakups:** NASA lists 22 known breakups between 1961 and 1969. Examples:
  - Sputnik 29 final stage (1962-057), 29 Oct 1962
  - Atlas Centaur 2 (1963-047), 27 Nov 1963
  - Kosmos 50 (1964-070), 5 Nov 1964: deliberate self-destruct
  - OV2-1/LCS 2 Titan transtage (1965-082), 15 Oct 1965: 473 fragments, the largest of the decade
  - OV2-3 transtage (1965-108), 21 Dec 1965: 102 of 109 fragments still in orbit
  - Kosmos 249 and Kosmos 252 (Oct–Nov 1968): deliberate destruction in Soviet anti-satellite tests
  - OPS 7613 Agena D (1969-082), 4 Oct 1969: 262 fragments
- **When tracking started:** the US Air Force's Project Space Track began in 1957. On 1 July 1961, two days after the Ablestar breakup, NORAD's space tracking system (SPADATS) went operational under the 1st Aerospace Surveillance and Control Squadron. These tracking dates come from Wikipedia and NORAD summaries, not primary records.

## 2. People
- The Applied Physics Laboratory at Johns Hopkins built the Transit navigation satellites for the US Navy. They are the ancestor of GPS-style satellite navigation.
- Aerojet-General built the Ablestar stage (AJ10-104S) and co-wrote the 1961 report on the breakup.
- Phillip Anz-Meador, John Opiela and J.-C. Liou of NASA's Orbital Debris Program Office compile the history of breakups.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- NASA's 1985 report says the event sharply jumped the number of tracked objects. A secondary source says it more than tripled known satellites. That fits our catalogue: before this launch, catalogue numbers only reached about 115.
- The fix (vent leftover propellant) became a core rule for keeping orbits clean, decades before formal debris guidelines.
- Fragments from 1961 are still being found and catalogued. 21 pieces from this breakup have NORAD numbers above 40,000, i.e. were catalogued in the last decade or so.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "This is the first known satellite fragmentation." NASA ODPO, *History of On-orbit Satellite Fragmentations*, 16th ed., 2022.
- "…several objects from the very first breakup in 1961 being added to the catalog since the previous edition…" Same report.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"Kessler or Iridium–Kosmos was the first debris problem."** No. Debris dates from 1961.
- **"It was a collision."** No. It was a propellant explosion. Nearly all 1960s breakups were explosions or deliberate destruction.
- **"The Transit 4A satellite exploded."** No. The *rocket stage* (NORAD 118) broke up, and the satellites survived.
- **Fragment counts change every edition** (294 in older sources, 317 in 2022). Always give the year.
