# 20 · CubeSats: the 10 cm satellite (1999–2006)

## 1. What happened
- **1999:** Jordi Puig-Suari of Cal Poly in San Luis Obispo and Bob Twiggs of Stanford's Space Systems Development Laboratory created the CubeSat standard (NASA *CubeSat 101*; Puig-Suari's own biography). The basic unit (1U) is a 10 × 10 × 10 cm cube weighing at most 1.33 kg. Cal Poly built the P-POD, a spring-loaded box that launches three units, and still maintains the specification.
- **30 June 2003:** a Rockot (a converted Soviet ICBM, sold by Eurockot) launched from **Plesetsk** carrying the first CubeSats as secondary payloads alongside Russia's Monitor-E and several larger satellites. Five 1U CubeSats shared one P-POD, and QuakeSat (3U) had its own. Results (eoPortal):
  - **XI-IV** (University of Tokyo): worked, and was still confirmed operating in December 2020.
  - **CUTE-I** (Tokyo Institute of Technology): worked.
  - **AAU CubeSat** (Aalborg, Denmark): ran about 2.5 months with a weak transmitter.
  - **DTUSat** (Technical University of Denmark): never contacted.
  - **CanX-1** (University of Toronto): never contacted.
  - **QuakeSat** (Stanford / QuakeFinder): flew about 18 months.
- **Norway, nCube:** two 1U CubeSats built by students at NTNU, the University of Oslo, Narvik University College and NMBU. Their payload was an AIS receiver to pick up ship signals, and a test was planned to track reindeer fitted with AIS collars.
  - **nCube-2** launched 27 October 2005 from Plesetsk on a Kosmos-3M, released from ESA's student satellite SSETI Express. **No signal was ever received**; SNL suggests it may have stayed stuck in its carrier.
  - **nCube-1** was lost when a Dnepr rocket from Baikonur failed on **26 July 2006**.
- SNL notes that Norway has had student satellite projects since 2001, and that nCube helped lay "grunnlaget" for AISSat-1 (see beat 23).

## 2. People
- **Jordi Puig-Suari:** Cal Poly professor; wrote the specification and led the P-POD; co-founded Tyvak in 2011.
- **Bob Twiggs:** Stanford professor. He chose the 10 cm size after buying a clear plastic display box that turned out to be just under 4 inches (Aviation Week, 2023).
- **Shinichi Nakasuka:** led the University of Tokyo lab (ISSL) behind XI-IV. I did not check this in a primary source this session, so confirm before using.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- Students could build a working satellite within a degree programme, on a university budget. The standard size let any CubeSat ride along on any rocket that carried a P-POD.
- The format later carried commercial fleets such as Planet's Doves (beat 24) and Norwegian maritime surveillance.

## 4. Primary-source quotes
- "The original intent of the project was to provide affordable access to space for the university science community." (NASA, *CubeSat 101*, 2017)
- Twiggs had "no idea that it would become an industry standard." This is a fragment quoted in Aviation Week (G. Reim, 8 Nov 2023), not his full sentence.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"The first CubeSat flew on a Dnepr."** Popular Science says so, but the first ones flew on a Rockot from Plesetsk. The Dnepr is the rocket that lost nCube-1 in 2006.
- **"Six of six worked."** Only four were ever heard from, and two never made contact.
- **"nCube-2 failed in orbit."** It probably never separated or powered up properly; no signal was ever heard.
- **"Invented in 2000" or "1998."** Sources disagree on when the collaboration started. NASA and Puig-Suari say 1999.
