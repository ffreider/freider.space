# 10 — Molniya: an orbit built for the north (1965)

## 1. What happened
- **Failed attempts:** 4 June 1964 (booster failure), then 22 August 1964. The August satellite reached orbit but its antennas did not deploy, and it was publicly named **Kosmos 41**. These details come from Wikipedia and should be checked against a Russian source.
- **Molniya 1-1:** launched 23 April 1965 from Tyuratam/Baikonur. This date is confirmed by russianspaceweb.com and in satcat.csv.
- **First broadcasts:** a test TV relay Moscow → Vladivostok followed within days, then Vladivostok → Moscow on 27 April 1965. Sources are Wikipedia and a 1968 DTIC report seen via search; verify both.
- **Orbita network:** in 1967 Molniya became the basis of the Orbita TV distribution network. russianspaceweb gives 7 November 1967 as the date it was declared operational, and another source gives 2 November. Flag the discrepancy.
- **The orbit:**
  - inclination 63.4°, eccentricity about 0.72 (NASA Earth Observatory)
  - period about 12 hours, i.e. two orbits a day
  - apogee about 40,000 km over the northern hemisphere, perigee a few hundred to about 2,000 km in the south
  - Example from the catalogue: Molniya 1-29 (1975) is in a 1,718 × 38,636 km orbit with a period of 717.8 minutes.
  - At 63.4° the apogee does not drift around the orbit, so it stays over the north.
  - The satellite moves slowly near apogee and spends most of each 12 hours high over the USSR. One satellite gave about 8 usable hours a day (DTIC, via search), so the network needed several satellites.

## 2. People
- **Sergei Korolev**'s OKB-1 began work on a communications satellite in 1961 (russianspaceweb).
- **NPO PM**, now ISS Reshetnev in Zheleznogorsk near Krasnoyarsk, developed Molniya-1 for Orbita. The handover from OKB-1 and the role of Mikhail Reshetnev are general knowledge, not checked here.

## 3. Why it mattered on the ground
- A geostationary satellite sits over the equator, so from far north it sits on the horizon. NASA Earth Observatory says such locations "are always on the edge of view". My own geometry calculation for a satellite due south on the same longitude, not from a source:
  - Moscow (55.8° N): about 26° above the horizon
  - Andenes (69.3° N): about 12°
  - Longyearbyen (78.2° N): about 3°
  - Above about 81.3° N: below the horizon
- Launching into an equatorial orbit from a launch site at about 46° N costs a lot of energy. russianspaceweb says "limitations of available rocket power" pushed OKB-1 toward this orbit.
- Orbita brought Moscow TV to Siberia and the Far East. Many viewers in remote regions got central television for the first time.
- **Norway today:** the same logic produced Space Norway's two ASBM satellites (launched August 2024). They are in a highly elliptical orbit that reaches about 44,000 km over the Arctic and are aimed at coverage north of about 65–68° N.

## 6. Commonly misstated
- **"The Soviets couldn't reach geostationary orbit."** They later did, with Raduga and Ekran from 1975 onward. Molniya was the cheaper and better-suited choice for the high north.
- **"Molniya means a type of orbit."** The orbit is named after the satellites. *Molniya* is Russian for "lightning".
- **"A Molniya satellite hovers over Russia."** No. It lingers near apogee for hours and then sweeps fast through perigee, so a constellation is needed for round-the-clock service.
